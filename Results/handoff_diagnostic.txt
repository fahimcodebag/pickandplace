================================================================================
HANDOFF-POSE DIAGNOSTIC
Why does an identical transport policy fail 5x more often depending on which
grasp policy feeds it?
================================================================================

QUESTION
The end-to-end ladder held the transport policy BYTE-IDENTICAL across four arms,
yet transport_stall ranged 13 -> 73 per 400 episodes and timeout_dropped
73 -> 24. Something in the handoff state, not the transport weights, is driving
this. handoff_diag.py records the object's pose IN THE GRIPPER FRAME (and, in
round 2, end-effector position and gripper width) at the moment the grasp stage
hands off, then the episode outcome. 400 episodes per arm (4 seeds x 100).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 1 -- THE CORNER GRIP. Confirmed, large, actionable.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The predictive feature is not raw relative yaw but distance from a flat face:
diag = min(|rel_yaw|, 90 - |rel_yaw|). The bread is a box -- at 0 or 90 degrees
the fingers close on flat faces, at ~45 degrees they close on CORNERS.

  grip angle        baseline   gripfix_s0   gripfix_s2
  flat   ( 0-15)      74.7%       72.2%       92.6%
  mid    (15-30)      71.4%       59.9%       81.7%
  corner (30-45)      54.7%       45.7%       70.2%
  p (failures more diagonal)  4.1e-3      4.9e-5      9.8e-8

Monotonic in all three arms. Cost of a corner grip: 20-26 points of end-to-end
success. Prevalence: 94/400 = 24%, IDENTICAL across arms -- object rotation is
uniform and no policy compensates for it.

The damage is done through drops, not stalls:
  drop rate flat -> corner:  baseline 11.3 -> 25.3, s0 3.0 -> 17.0, s2 1.7 -> 7.4

WHY THE REWARD ALLOWS IT
A corner grip passes lift certification. It survives a straight vertical pull,
so W_GRASP_SUCCESS pays in full. It fails only later, under the lateral
accelerations of transport. The grasp policy commands wrist yaw through
OSC_POSE -- it CAN align to a face; it has no incentive to.

Headroom: gripfix_s2 scores 92.6% on flat grips against 81.0% overall.
Eliminating corner grips is worth ~+9 points end-to-end with no change to the
transport policy.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 2 -- STALLS ARE NOT EXPLAINED BY OBJECT-IN-GRIPPER POSE. Hypothesis
refuted.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Restricting to FLAT grips only, where handoff object pose is statistically
indistinguishable between the two policies:

  stall rate on flat grips:   gripfix_s0 16.0%   gripfix_s2 2.3%

A 7x gap with the same transport policy and the same grip geometry. Within s0,
stalls are not separated from successes by lateral offset (p=0.38), object
height (p=0.61), gripper width (p=0.21) or handoff attempts (p=0.21) -- only by
grip angle, weakly (p=0.02).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FINDING 3 -- s0's TRANSPORT DEGRADES WITH DISTANCE; s2's DOES NOT.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Stall rate vs Euclidean distance from the end-effector to the target bin
(bin at x=0.198, y=0.158) at the moment of handoff:

  dist (m)      gripfix_s0            gripfix_s2
                stall   success       stall   success
  0.00-0.35     16.3%    61.2%         2.6%    79.2%
  0.35-0.45     16.5%    72.9%         5.9%    81.2%
  0.45-0.55     21.1%    69.7%         3.2%    83.1%
  0.55-1.00     42.6%    37.7%         2.6%    89.5%
  p(stall vs success)  4.2e-4              2.9e-1 (ns)

s0 collapses as the carry gets longer. s2 is flat, and its success rate
actually RISES with distance.

Critically, this is NOT a distance effect: s2 hands off FARTHER on average
(median 0.480 m) than s0 (0.369 m) and stalls 7x less. Distance is the axis
along which s0's defect becomes visible, not the cause.

INTERPRETATION (hypothesis, not established)
A per-episode outlier pose would show up as a tail. This shows up as a
distribution-wide bias that compounds with path length -- consistent with s0
handing off with a small systematic error in the held-object configuration, so
transport's commanded direction is slightly off and the path curves. Over a
short carry it arrives before the 50-step no-new-best stall timer trips; over a
long carry it does not. Not verified.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MEASUREMENT LIMITATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Round 2 intended to test arm posture, but robosuite does not expose
"robot0_joint_pos" -- it publishes robot0_joint_pos_cos / _sin. The dict lookup
silently returned its zero default, so all seven joint columns are zeros and
JOINT CONFIGURATION REMAINS UNTESTED. The Panda is 7-DOF and redundant, so two
policies can present an object identically while leaving the arm in different
joint postures; transport's observation includes proprioception, making this a
live hypothesis for Finding 3. End-effector position, which WAS captured, is
what Finding 3 rests on.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT TO DO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. RECOMMENDED -- add a wrist-alignment term to the grasp reward penalising
   min(|rel_yaw|, 90-|rel_yaw|) at gripper close. Attacks a 24%-prevalence,
   20-point failure at its source. Finding 1 is established across three
   independent policies.
2. NOT RECOMMENDED -- retraining transport on the wider handoff distribution.
   Already attempted: best retrained seed 75% vs 82% untouched
   (Results/transport_retrain_negative.txt).

Note that Findings 2 and 3 concern gripfix_s0, which is NOT the selected
policy. gripfix_s2 stalls at 2.6-5.9% with no distance dependence at all, so
the stall pathology is largely absent from the chosen pipeline. Finding 1 is
the one that applies to s2 and carries the remaining headroom.
